# Pitch facts: what we can say, and where it comes from

Checked on 21 November 2026. Each figure was read in the paper, its PubMed/PMC abstract, or the publisher's own page, unless the label says otherwise.

Labels:

- **CONFIRMED FROM PRIMARY SOURCE** — we opened the paper or its official abstract and saw the number.
- **SECONDARY ONLY** — we saw the number only in another paper, a search summary or an organisation's fact page.
- **COULD NOT VERIFY** — do not say it.

Rule for slides: use the "safe to say" wording. The exact numbers are fragile; small changes in wording turn a true statement into a false one (see thirst, below).

## 1. Communication problems and preventable harm

- **Source:** Bartlett G, Blais R, Tamblyn R, Clermont RJ, MacGibbon B. Impact of patient communication problems on the risk of preventable adverse events in acute care settings. *CMAJ* 2008;178(12):1555–1562.
- **URL:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2396356/ (abstract: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18519903/)
- **Exact figure:** odds ratio 3.00, 95% confidence interval 1.43–6.27.
- **Sample:** 2,355 randomly selected patient charts from 20 general hospitals in Quebec. 217 adverse events were found; 63 (29%) were judged preventable.
- **Safe to say:** "A Quebec study of 2,355 hospital charts found that patients with a communication problem had three times the odds of a preventable adverse event."
- **Caveats:** "Communication problem" meant any documented difficulty communicating: language barriers, deafness, blindness and other physical causes. It is not a study of patients who cannot speak, and not an ICU study. The confidence interval is wide. Say "three times the odds", not "three times as many" and not "causes".
- **Status:** CONFIRMED FROM PRIMARY SOURCE. It is also a Canadian study published in a Canadian journal, which suits a pitch in London, Ontario.

## 2. Pain communication in the ICU

- **Source:** Happ MB, Garrett K, Thomas DD, Tate J, George E, Houze M, Radtke J, Sereika S. Nurse-patient communication interactions in the intensive care unit. *American Journal of Critical Care* 2011;20(2):e28–e40.
- **URL:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3222584/
- **Exact figures:** "Messages about pain were in this 'unsuccessful' range 37.7% of the time." Patients rated 40% of communication sessions as somewhat to extremely difficult (the abstract says 40%; the results section says 38%).
- **Sample:** 10 nurses and 30 patients in two ICUs at one centre, 120 video-recorded observation sessions, 943 communication exchanges.
- **Safe to say:** "In a video study of 30 ICU patients who could not speak, more than a third of exchanges about pain did not get through."
- **Caveats:** Small sample, one hospital system. Overall, more than 70% of exchanges were successful, so do not imply that most communication fails. The paper describes the patients as nonspeaking; confirm the word "ventilated" in the paper's methods before using it on a slide.
- **Status:** CONFIRMED FROM PRIMARY SOURCE.

**Bonus fact from the same paper (CONFIRMED):** "No communication boards, alphabet boards, or picture boards were observed in use during these video-recorded observations." Safe to say: "Across 120 observed sessions, nobody used a communication board."

## 3. How many ventilated patients could use a communication tool

- **Source:** Happ MB, Seaman JB, Nilsen ML, Sciulli A, Tate JA, Saul M, Barnato AE. The number of mechanically ventilated ICU patients meeting communication criteria. *Heart & Lung* 2015;44(1):45–49.
- **URL:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5935242
- **Exact figure:** 1,440 of 2,671 patients (53.9%) met basic communication criteria.
- **Sample:** 2,671 patients ventilated for two days or more, six specialty ICUs in two Pittsburgh hospitals, August 2009 to July 2011. "Met criteria" means awake, alert and responsive to clinicians for at least one 12-hour shift while on the ventilator.
- **Safe to say:** "In a study of 2,671 ventilated patients, more than half were awake and responsive enough that a communication tool could have served them."
- **Caveat:** PLAN.md says they "could communicate if given a tool". The paper does not claim that. It says they "could potentially be served by assistive communication tools and speech-language consultation." Keep "could have been served" or "were awake enough to".
- **Status:** CONFIRMED FROM PRIMARY SOURCE, with the wording above.

## 4. Thirst in the ICU — PLAN.md has this one wrong

- **Source:** Puntillo KA, Arai S, Cohen NH, Gropper MA, Neuhaus J, Paul SM, Miaskowski C. Symptoms experienced by intensive care unit patients at high risk of dying. *Critical Care Medicine* 2010;38(11):2155–2160.
- **URL:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3377582/
- **What the paper says:** Patients reported thirst in 70.8% of 405 symptom assessments. That is prevalence. Separately, "Thirst was the most intense and confusion was the least intense symptom" (mean intensity 2.16 on a 1–3 scale, the highest of ten symptoms).
- **What PLAN.md says:** "70.8% of ICU patients rated thirst their most intense symptom." That merges two findings into one that the paper does not report. At least one later review repeats the same merged wording, which is probably where it came from.
- **Sample:** 171 patients who could self-report, 405 assessments, two ICUs at one US hospital. All were patients at high risk of dying, not ICU patients in general.
- **Safe to say:** "In a study of 171 seriously ill ICU patients, thirst was reported in 7 out of 10 symptom checks, and it was the most intense of the ten symptoms measured."
- **Status:** CONFIRMED FROM PRIMARY SOURCE for the reworded claim.

This is the best single fact for the demo, because the first thing the patient asks for is water.

## 5. Cost of dedicated eye-gaze communication devices

- **Claim in PLAN.md:** US$7,000–15,000.
- **What we found:** Tobii Dynavox does not publish prices for the TD I-13 or I-16; its US page offers a quote instead (https://us.tobiidynavox.com/pages/td-i-series). We found no manufacturer price list that supports the range.
- **The one number we could verify:** Virginia Medicaid's 2026 equipment list sets the fee for code E2510, "speech generating device, synthesized speech, permitting multiple methods of message formulation and multiple methods of device access", at **US$8,681.12**. Eye-tracking accessories are billed separately under E2599 and priced case by case. Source: https://dmas.virginia.gov/media/mnypcbg3/appendix-b-communication-devices-2026.pdf
- **Safe to say:** "One US state Medicaid programme pays about US$8,700 for a speech device of this class, before the eye-tracking accessory is added."
- **Do not say:** "$7,000 to $15,000" or "$15,000" as fact. If a judge asks, say that manufacturers quote privately and the figure above is a public payer rate.
- **Status:** range COULD NOT VERIFY; the Medicaid figure is CONFIRMED FROM PRIMARY SOURCE.

## 6. Who this could help: ALS and locked-in syndrome

**ALS, United States — CONFIRMED FROM PRIMARY SOURCE (CDC's page for the paper)**

- Mehta P, Raymond J, Punjani R, et al. Prevalence of amyotrophic lateral sclerosis in the United States using established and novel methodologies, 2017. *Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis and Frontotemporal Degeneration* (doi 10.1080/21678421.2022.2059380).
- URL: https://www.cdc.gov/als/php/publications/prevalence-of-amyotrophic-lateral-sclerosis-in-the-united-states-using-established-and-novel.html
- Figures: an estimated 24,821 cases in 2017, or 7.7 per 100,000 people, after correcting for missed cases. The registry's traditional method gives 5.5 per 100,000; the upper estimate is 9.9.
- Safe to say: "The CDC estimates about 25,000 people in the United States were living with ALS in 2017."

**ALS, Canada — SECONDARY ONLY**

- ALS Society of Canada: about 4,000 Canadians live with ALS and about 1,000 are diagnosed each year. https://als.ca/what-is-als/als-overview/
- This is the charity's own figure with no method shown. Safe to say: "ALS Canada estimates that about 4,000 Canadians are living with ALS."

**Locked-in syndrome — CONFIRMED FROM PRIMARY SOURCE**

- Pels EGM, Aarnoutse EJ, Ramsey NF, Vansteensel MJ. Estimated prevalence of the target population for brain-computer interface neurotechnology in the Netherlands. *Neurorehabilitation and Neural Repair* 2017;31(7):677–685. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6396873
- Figure: 0.73 per 100,000 inhabitants, extrapolated from 26 patients reported by Dutch family doctors. Only 21% of doctors replied, and the authors say the estimate could be off in either direction. The definition is functional and includes late-stage ALS, not only brainstem stroke.
- Safe to say: "Locked-in syndrome is rare: one Dutch study estimated fewer than one person in 100,000."
- Do not multiply this up to a Canadian or world total. Nobody measured that.

**Framing advice.** ALS and locked-in syndrome are small groups. The larger group is temporary: ventilated ICU patients (fact 3). Lead with the ICU, mention ALS and locked-in syndrome as the long-term users.

## 7. Further verified facts

**Frustration (CONFIRMED FROM PRIMARY SOURCE)**

- Patak L, Gawlinski A, Fung NI, Doering L, Berg J, Henneman EA. Communication boards in critical care: patients' views. *Applied Nursing Research* 2006;19(4):182–190. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17098155/
- "Sixty-two percent (n = 18) of patients reported a high level of frustration" in communicating their needs while ventilated. "Most patients (69%; n = 20) perceived that a communication board would have been helpful."
- Sample: 29 patients interviewed within 72 hours of the breathing tube coming out. One hospital.
- Safe to say: "In interviews with 29 patients just off a ventilator, 18 described a high level of frustration in trying to communicate."
- Say the 29. A percentage from 29 people sounds bigger than it is.

**A commercial eye-based device has been piloted in the ICU (CONFIRMED FROM PRIMARY SOURCE)**

- Bendavid I, Assi S, Sasson N, et al. The EyeControl-Med device, an alternative tool for communication in ventilated critically ill patients: a pilot study examining communication capabilities and delirium. *Journal of Critical Care* 2023. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37348187/
- 15 ventilated or tracheotomised patients, single arm, funded by the manufacturer. Communication scores improved by a mean of 19.3 points on the Loewenstein Communication Scale. The authors say controlled studies are still needed.
- Why it matters to us: it shows hospitals will trial eye-based communication at the bedside, and that the evidence bar is a clinical study, which we have not done.

**Not to be used**

- Hurtig, Alper & Berkowitz 2018 (*Perspectives of the ASHA Special Interest Groups* 3(12):99–112) estimate that removing communication barriers could prevent 671,440 adverse events and save US$6.8 billion a year in the US. We could not open the paper (PMC blocked automated access), and the numbers are a model built on the Bartlett odds ratio, not a measurement. SECONDARY ONLY.
- Zubow & Hurtig 2013, "33% of conscious ventilated ICU patients cannot communicate with caregivers" and "14% of conscious hospital patients cannot use the nurse call": seen only in a university web article. COULD NOT VERIFY.
- Rotondi et al. 2002 on patients being bothered by not being able to talk: the item-level percentage is not in the abstract. COULD NOT VERIFY.
- We found no solid, citable number on nurse call response times. Do not quote one.

## 8. Closest prior work, and how this project differs

**"Eid et al. 2016" — the citation in PLAN.md is misnamed. SECONDARY ONLY.**

- The paper is by Simone **Eidam**, Jens Garstka and Gabriele Peters (University of Hagen), presented at COGNITIVE 2016 in Rome. "Eid" appears to come from the PDF's file name. A search result gives the title as "Towards Regaining Mobility Through Virtual Presence for Patients with Locked-in Syndrome"; we could not open the PDF to confirm the title (the university's site refused automated access), so check it before citing. Link: https://fernuni-hagen.de/mci/download/pub/c2016_eidetal_cognitive.pdf ; group page: https://www.fernuni-hagen.de/mci/research/interactive-systems/impaired.shtml
- From the paper's abstract as shown in search results: a prototype interface for locked-in patients, controlled by eye gestures, in which the patient selects objects in a picture of their surroundings; "the object recognition is simulated".
- Difference, in one sentence: their prototype simulated the object recognition and had the user select objects in an image on a screen, while ours has the patient look at the physical object, confirms with a held YES, and delivers the request to a nurse station.

**SceneTalk — CONFIRMED FROM PRIMARY SOURCE (we read the paper).**

- Kane SK, Morris MR. Let's Talk About X: Combining Image Recognition and Eye Gaze to Support Conversation for People with ALS. *DIS 2017*, Edinburgh, pp. 129–134. doi 10.1145/3064663.3064762. https://microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2017/04/scenetalk.pdf
- What it is: an on-screen eye-typing keyboard that takes a photo with the device's rear camera, sends it to a cloud image-recognition service, and adds words and stock phrases about the recognised objects to the prediction bar. Six people with ALS gave feedback; the authors state that a field deployment was beyond the scope of the work.
- Difference: SceneTalk speeds up typing on a screen for people who already use an eye-typing device and relies on a cloud service, while ours needs no typing, asks one yes/no question about the object being looked at, and routes the answer to a nurse.
- Note: PLAN.md describes this work as having "simulated recognition or on-screen selection". For SceneTalk the recognition was real; the on-screen selection part is accurate.

**EyeControl (EyeControl-Med) — CONFIRMED for the description; clearance claims not checked.**

- A head-worn camera tracks the eye, and the patient hears audio prompts and answers with eye movements; no screen. Pilot study above (15 patients). EU project summary: https://cordis.europa.eu/article/id/442638-innovative-platform-helps-acute-care-patients-communicate
- Difference: EyeControl is a wearable with audio menus that has been piloted with real ICU patients, while ours is contact-free, puts nothing on the patient's head, and starts from objects in the room; it has not been tested with any patient.
- We did not check the FDA or CE status ourselves. Do not state it.

**Tobii Dynavox (TD I-Series) — CONFIRMED from the manufacturer's page.**

- A dedicated speech-generating device that "can be controlled with one's eyes"; a full communication system with a precise screen-mounted eye tracker. https://us.tobiidynavox.com/pages/td-i-series
- Difference: a Tobii Dynavox device gives a trained user full language, while ours covers only a handful of basic needs with a webcam and is meant as a first layer for patients who do not have such a device, or cannot yet use one.

**Google Look to Speak — CONFIRMED from Google's announcement (8 December 2020).**

- An Android app: "people simply have to look left, right or up to quickly select what they want to say from a list of phrases", and the phone speaks the phrase. https://blog.google/outreach-initiatives/accessibility/look-to-speak/
- Difference: Look to Speak narrows down a phrase list by repeated left/right choices and speaks to whoever is in the room, while ours picks the topic from where the patient looks in the room, needs one yes/no, and sends the request to a nurse who is not in the room, with acknowledgement and escalation.
- Be straightforward that our YES/NO step uses the same left/right idea.
